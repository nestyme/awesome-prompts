# Creator Negotiation

What to do after a creator answers "my rate is X". This is the money half of creator outreach; [influencer-outreach](../influencer-outreach/SKILL.md) finds the creators and writes the first email, and this skill takes over at the first reply with a number in it.

The rules come from a multi-month run (Aug–Nov 2026) across Europe, the Americas, Asia and Africa in which every negotiation was logged with the creator's quote, our reply, and the outcome, and from what creators say publicly about how brands treat them. Our numbers are given as percentages and ratios from that log; the external sources are in [references/evidence.md](references/evidence.md).

_Skill by [@nestymee](https://x.com/nestymee)._

## Ground rules

**No tactics.** No invented deadlines, no made-up competing offers, no pretending to be a bigger company or a different person, no "other creators took less", and no taking advantage of a misunderstanding (currency especially). Every lever in this skill is a true fact about you or the deal. Beyond ethics, there are two practical reasons. Creators talk to each other. And at micro scale the relationship is the asset: the creator who turns down your number this month is the one you'll want when the budget grows, and in our log most declines ended with the creator asking us to come back.

**Draft, never send.** A person reads and sends every message. Negotiation is where an automated mistake costs the most: a wrong currency, a second counter, a tone slip.

## Before you reply, work out three numbers

Do this before reading their quote closely, so their anchor doesn't move yours.

1. **Expected views of the sponsored video.** A sponsored video got about a third of the creator's unpinned median in our log (we saw 15% to 150%). Accounts whose median came from one viral hit got about 2%. Recompute the median from the last five unpinned videos this week; quoted case studies and old medians describe an account that may no longer exist.
2. **Where those views land.** Ask for the audience-by-country screenshot before paying (message 5 in the skeletons). Creators sent it within a day and none took offense. This decides *whether* to buy, not how hard to haggle: views from countries where your product doesn't sell are worth close to nothing at any price. In our log, one video brought a burst of installs from a country where the app doesn't sell and none of them paid; another brought a wave of users from Spain and LatAm, and only a small fraction of them paid, far below the US rate.
3. **Your ceiling per video** = expected views from paying countries ÷ 1,000 × the most you'll pay per thousand. Our cost per thousand views varied more than 50× between deals. The cheapest ones were a single fee that covered the same video on TikTok, Reels and Shorts. Published market CPMs disagree with each other by more than 40× because they define CPM differently, so set your own number from your own payback, not from a rate report.

For a sense of the market: on one large marketplace about 80% of creator deals are under $300, and TikTok creators were paid roughly their listed price. Cold-email quotes run higher than marketplace listings, which is why practitioners still expect to land 15–30% below the first quote, and report that asking for 40% or more off gets refusals or visibly weaker videos.

## Read the quote

Convert a package to a per-video price, then take **ratio = your ceiling ÷ their per-video ask**.

| Ratio | What happened in our log | Do |
|---|---|---|
| ≥ 1 | Nearly always closed | Accept. Don't haggle a price you can afford; pay fast instead. |
| ~0.6–1 | Mostly closed, often after one halfway step (they ask 100, you offer 60, they come back at 80, you say yes) | Accept, or make one counter and meet halfway once if they come back. |
| ~0.33–0.6 | Closed when we cut scope; stalled when we only cut the price | One counter that trades scope: one test video instead of three, no cross-post, a shorter series. |
| < 0.33 | Almost never closed. The one exception was a creator new to paid deals who took 25% of her ask as a single "first test". | Don't counter. Decline warmly and leave the door open. |

Two cases skip the table:

- **They gave a series price.** Take it. Creators typically discounted themselves 10–35% for a series. When we accepted a creator's own package price, all but one deal closed (the exception went quiet before signing), usually at the best per-video rate we got anywhere. A number people set themselves sticks. That's why the first email should ask for both a single-video and a series price.
- **Agency, talent manager or UGC studio rate sheet.** Fixed sheets (plus 30% for posting, plus 30% for usage rights) that run 3–10× what a direct creator quotes. None of them closed. Send one polite decline.

Every closed deal in our log settled within two rounds: their ask, our counter, their counter, and at most one final step from us, usually a scope change. If you're about to go past that, stop.

## Write the reply

- **A real person, first name, true situation.** "We're two people building this app" is a fact; use it if it's true for you. It didn't raise first-email reply rates in our data, but creators quoted it back when they accepted a lower number.
- **In their language.** Spanish intros got about twice the reply rate of English ones. If you write through a translator, say so. When a creator wanted to negotiate on a call, telling him "I write with a translator, so writing works better for me" kept the deal and moved it to email.
- **In the currency they used.** If they quoted in COP, MXN, CLP, DKK or CAD, answer in it; if they quoted in USD, stay in USD (many LatAm creators prefer being paid in dollars). In Mexico and much of LatAm, "$" often means local pesos: ask (message 4) rather than take the reading that suits you. In one case the two readings differed almost 20×; we asked, it was pesos, and the deal closed on trust. If PayPal can't pay out in their currency (COP and ZAR among others), say up front that you'll send USD at the day's rate and cover the fees.
- **One honest number with its reason.** The reason is your budget or your stage, never a judgment of them. Don't write "your views don't support that rate".
- **Say their rate is fair** when you think it is. Usually it is; you just can't afford it.
- **The easy out, in the same message.** "If it doesn't work for you, no hard feelings at all."
- **Only real value on top of the money**: full payment upfront before filming, fees covered, no script and no draft approval, no usage rights taken, creative freedom, premium access to the product, a personal promo code for their audience, quick replies. Creators who accepted below their rate named these back to us, upfront payment and creative freedom most often.
- **Trade scope, not small price steps.** Dropping the cross-post or starting with one test video meets them; shaving $10 three times reads as haggling.

## Paid extras: don't slip them in

Link in bio, usage rights or paid-ad whitelisting, exclusivity, extra stories, several revision rounds, a script: each is its own line on a creator's rate card. Adding them "while we're at it" is what turns a fair offer into a lowball. Cross-posting the same video to Reels or Shorts is the one exception, since small creators often include it or charge little for it. Ask for it in the offer, and drop it first when you need to trade scope.

**Performance bonuses** are fine as upside and never a substitute for a guaranteed base. Creators with a fixed rate pushed back on bonus-heavy offers ("my base would need to be guaranteed, upfront"). At micro scale, bonuses paid per 25k views rarely trigger, so don't present one as real money. If you accept a creator's own fixed fee, don't bolt a bonus on top.

## When to stop

Close the thread kindly when any of these is true:

- The ratio is under about a third after an honest look at your budget.
- They answered your honest number with the same rate ("I'd prefer to keep my minimum"). Don't send a second number.
- They talk in "starting at", "my minimum", or a manager's rate sheet.
- Their audience is mostly in countries that don't pay for your product. A lower price doesn't fix geography.
- They went silent after your counter. Send one check-in after 5–7 days with explicit permission to say no, then close. In our log, 94% of all replies came before any follow-up, and the few negotiations that stalled right after our counter never came back. (A check-in does work later in the deal, when an agreement is waiting for a signature: people get busy.)
- You've each had two moves and there's still a gap.

The closing message thanks them, gives one sentence of reason (your stage, not their price), and invites them back. Mean it: keep a "revisit when the budget grows" list with the date and their rate, and actually write to them when it does.

## How not to offend

Creators mostly resent how a brand behaves; the size of the number matters less. The creator-side surveys and our own replies point at the same list:

- **Slow or late payment.** It's the most common creator complaint: in one survey 56% had been paid late, and net-60 terms are still normal at big brands. Paying upfront, the same day, is the cheapest respect you can show, and it buys flexibility on the rate.
- **Feeling undervalued.** 74% of creators in the same survey had stopped working with a brand over it. A low number with a true reason ("it's our budget at this stage") lands; a low number with a judgment ("your views don't justify it") doesn't. Never compare them to another creator's price.
- **Changing the deal after yes.** One in three marketers say renegotiating deliverables put a creator off entirely. Everything you want goes in the first offer.
- **Extras slipped in for free**: usage rights and whitelisting (each usually +30–50%), exclusivity, a rush timeline under two weeks, a link in bio (sold per week or per month), extra revision rounds. See the section above.
- **Control.** Scripts, "unlimited revisions", approval of every draft. The norm is up to two rounds of minor edits; a reshoot is a new deliverable. We ask for one round, only for factual mistakes about the product.
- **Guarantees and commission-only pay.** Most marketers who asked for minimum-view guarantees say it scared the creator off, and creators' openness to affiliate-only deals has dropped sharply. Lead with a flat base.
- **Product instead of money.** Free access to your product is a nice add-on, never the payment.
- **Copy-paste outreach.** Creators treat generic brand DMs as spam or a scam. A specific line about their content and a real name show that a person wrote it.
- **Ghosting.** Close every thread, including the ones you're declining. A two-line "this isn't a fit for our budget, thank you" is what makes the later "we've grown, can we talk?" possible.
- **Inconsistent prices.** Creators compare notes, and rate-sharing is spreading. Have one logic for your numbers (expected views × your price per thousand) and you can explain it the same way to anyone.

## After the yes

- **Confirm in writing.** A one-page agreement that restates what you already agreed, confirmed by "I agree" in the thread. Include ad disclosure the local way (the platform's paid-partnership label; in Korea vague tags like "AD" or "Collaboration" don't satisfy the regulator).
- **Pay the same day**, in the agreed amount and currency, with fees on you. Everyone who closed in our log was paid upfront, either in full or per video before each one.
- **No new asks after agreement.** Small fixes that make the deal work (the code in the caption, a tag) are fine. Anything that costs them reach or effort, like a link in bio, is a new negotiation.
- **Deadlines.** Check in once around the agreed date. Creators who confirmed a date kept it within a few days.
- **Payment method requests** (bank transfer, Interac). Offer your default first with its real advantage, then accommodate (message 10).
- **Per-video deals** let you see video one before paying for video two. Say so plainly (message 11). It's only honest if the agreement already says payment is per video.

## Two worked examples

Both assume you'll pay at most $20 per 1,000 views from countries where your product sells.

**"My rate is $500 per TikTok, or $1,300 for a series of three."** Unpinned median 9,000, audience 70% in countries where you sell. Expected views ≈ 9,000 ÷ 3 = 3,000, of which ~2,100 count. Ceiling ≈ $42 per video. Their package works out to about $430 per video, a ratio of 0.1. Decline warmly (message 6). A token counter here only adds a round and usually ends in the same kind no.

**"$300 per video, $800 for three."** Unpinned median 30k, audience 90% in countries where you sell. Expected views ≈ 10k, ~9k count. Ceiling ≈ $180. Ratio = 0.6 against the single-video price. Counter once, a little under the ceiling, and offer one test video instead of the series (message 2). If they come back above the ceiling, meet them at the ceiling by giving up scope, for example by dropping the cross-post. That's how deals in this band closed for us.

## Log every price

The ratio table, the series discount and the round limit are only as good as the log behind them, and yours will differ from ours. Store each price either side names as its own row, with the three numbers you believed at that moment, and recompute the table every few dozen negotiations. The schema, the views that recompute each rule, and the checks worth enforcing in code (no counter on an unconfirmed currency, one check-in, round limit): [references/crm-schema.md](references/crm-schema.md).

## Output

When the user brings a creator reply, return:

1. The three numbers (expected views, audience note, your ceiling) and the ratio, with any assumption marked.
2. A recommendation in one line: accept, counter (with the scope trade), meet halfway, decline, or close.
3. The draft reply in the creator's language, plus an English or user-language translation if the user doesn't read it.
4. Anything to check before sending: currency, audience screenshot, payment rails, a promise in the draft that must be true first (e.g. "payment sent").
